**RULES:**

(1) **Do not introduce anything that is factually incorrect into the defence.**

(2) If you argue that something is "standard," **you must justify that standard**-sometimes low standards **IS** the problem.

(3) If you argue that a choice is "conservative," **just must explain the logic for why this is the case**.

**TYPES OF ISSUE**

There are eight different types of issue:

**(Type A) Red Team mistake**
- These are the issues where the Red Team's critique depends on a mistake in their description.
- Check that they read the text correctly and have not invented an issue.
- Ensure that they have not invented an issue due to defects in their training data.
- **THE RED TEAM MUST NOT BE ALLOWED TO MAKE ANY MISTAKES.**
- **THE RED TEAM MUST NOT BE ALLOWED TO MISREPRESENT THE TEXT.**
- **Type A issues are the most important type of issue to flag.**
- **The most damaging Type A is the cheap shot:** an attack that the text's own definitions, stated conditions, or wording deflect in a single line. Apply this test to every issue — *would it survive the author simply pointing at their own text?* If the paper's own apparatus dissolves the issue, the Red Team has attacked a claim the text does not make, and you must flag it.
- **ANY** mistake in the Red Team's description must be called out.
- In quantitative texts, any regression results must be correctly interpreted; e.g., look for these kinds of errors when **any** issue involves a regression:
    - Focusing on the reduced form rather than IV coefficient in a 2SLS regression.
    - Mixing coefficients from different regression specifications.
    - Ignoring log transformations.
    - Interpreting logit as if they were OLS.
    - Misinterpreting interaction terms (e.g., treating the coefficient on an interaction as the full marginal effect, or ignoring that constituent terms' interpretation changes)
- Also be careful when the Red Team imputes any specific factual attributes to external sources cited in the text, such as:
    - Years or date ranges
    - Geographic coverage
    - Variables included or excluded
    - Sample restrictions
    - Numerical values
    - Data frequency
    - Methodological procedures
- It is probable that the Red Team has hallucinated these factual attributes to suit its argument.
- For mathematical claims (sign errors, wrong coefficients, incorrect derivations), check the Red Team's algebra carefully. If the Red Team has misquoted an equation or misidentified a variable's definition, flag it as Type A.
- When you flag a mathematical claim as Type A, **show your own derivation in the defence**, tracing each symbol to how the paper itself defines it. A bare assertion that the Red Team is wrong, or that the result is "standard", is not a defence.
- Flag any technical error. Specify whether the Red Team's critique depends on it **partly** or **entirely**.

**(Type B) Acknowledged issues**
- Any issue that the PDF itself recognizes as a potential limitation is an acknowledged issue.
- This does **not** mean that the PDF simply mentions X or Y.
- Rather,the PDF needs to **explicitly acknowledge that X or Y is a potential problem for their argument.**
- Examples:
    - "We acknowledge that X is a limitation..."
    - "We address concern Y in the robustness checks..."
- Be careful: **DO NOT CONFUSE TYPE B ISSUES WITH TYPE F ISSUES**, which are described below. **Just because you find a reference to something in the text, does not mean that the text itself recognizes it as an issue: it may be a feature rather than a bug.**
- **STRICT RULE: An issue is only Type B if the text EXPLICITLY acknowledges it as a limitation or problem for its own argument. A passing mention, a definition, or a robustness check that does not name the concern is NOT an acknowledgement and does NOT make the issue Type B.**
- **If the text instead RUNS A TEST that bears on the concern (a robustness check, placebo, alternative specification, alternative sample or control pool, or a formal statistical test), that is a Type H defence (below), NOT Type B. Type B is for verbal acknowledgement; Type H is for an empirical response.**

**(Type C) Clerical errors**
- Clerical errors are related to the presentation of information.
- Examples:
    - Sample size discrepancies
    - Unexplained data anomalies
    - Missing references
    - Totals that don't equal the sum
    - Mislabeled figures
- **We CANNOT know the impact of clerical errors, no matter how great they seem.**
- The principle of charity should be applied.

**(Type D) Structural errors**
- The following are **not** clerical errors:
    - Misinterpreted regression coefficients
    - Wrong functional forms
    - Mathematical contradictions in the model's own terms
    - Misinterpretations of theoretical concepts
    - Incorrect derivations
- These are structural issues and cannot be explained away as sloppiness because they threaten the logic of the text.

**(Type E) Visual evidence**
- Visual evidence is strictly forbidden: No issue can be based on the visual interpretation of a figure or plot, or any datapoint from within a figure or plot. Data must come from (a) the text, (b) tables, or (c) figure captions. Interpretations of figures or the extraction of datapoints from within figures are **strictly prohibited**. Do not use this kind of information. If the Red Team used this kind of information in its description, it should be flagged for deletion in the defence.
- **Any** issue that depends on this kind of visual evidence must be immediately dismissed.

**(Type F) Features-not-bugs**
- These issues are not issues at all, but are in fact **intrinsic parts of the text's argument**.
- The Red Team has **misunderstood the text**.
- For example, the Red Team has confused a part of the text's critique of the existing literature for a problem in the text. The Red Team have become confused and have assigned the flaw to the text itself.

**(Type G) Other**
- Any other type of issue.
- **IF THERE IS NO PLAUSIBLE DEFENCE, CLASSIFY IT AS OTHER.**

**(Type H) Empirically addressed**
- The text runs a specific empirical test that bears on the concern: a robustness check, placebo, falsification test, alternative specification, alternative sample or control pool, or a formal statistical test (e.g. a test of equality of coefficients, a pre-trend / parallel-trends test, randomization or permutation inference, a sensitivity bound).
- Unlike Type B, the text need **not** acknowledge the concern as a limitation. It is enough that it runs a test whose result speaks to the concern.
- **YOU MUST ACTIVELY AND EXHAUSTIVELY SEARCH the identification and robustness sections and the ENTIRE appendix — every supplementary table and figure caption — for these tests.** First build an inventory: list every robustness exhibit the author ran (pre-trend or parallel-trends tests, alternative specifications, alternative samples or control pools, placebo or falsification tests, sensitivity or bounding analyses, formal equality or interaction tests, inference corrections, external validation). Then, for EACH red-team concern, work down that inventory and cite EVERY exhibit that bears on it. A strong author answers a threat with an affirmative robustness exhibit, not a verbal admission, and these are the defences most often MISSED. Do not stop at the first exhibit; a single concern is often addressed by several, and missing one the author actually ran is a serious defect in the defence.
- For EVERY Type H defence you MUST give three things:
    1. **TEST:** the specific exhibit, with the table or section label the author gives it.
    2. **RESULT:** what the test shows, with the relevant numbers.
    3. **MECHANISM LINK:** why this test bears on the SPECIFIC causal pathway the Red Team's concern names. State the link explicitly; do not leave it implied.
- **Be honest about reach.** If the test addresses the concern only partially, or addresses a neighbouring threat rather than the exact one raised, SAY SO. A robustness check claimed to do more than it does invites a false dismissal at the next stage, which is worse than raising no defence at all.
