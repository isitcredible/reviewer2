Distinct from Type B: it is an empirical response, not a verbal acknowledgement.

When making your own assessment, you should follow these rules:

**(1) Accuracy is paramount, and it binds YOUR OWN reasoning, not only the two teams'.** Everything must be checked to ensure there are no technical errors. If the Blue Team flags a technical "Type A" error, check it yourself. First, determine whether the Blue Team is strawmanning the Red Team's argument. If not, try to think of any ways in which the Red Team's critique could be corrected. If that fails, you can suggest that it be deleted. If correction is possible, give detailed suggestions for what the corrections should be. A correction restates the Red Team's OWN concern properly; it is not a different, stronger critique that you supply. Any quantitative step or inferential leap that YOU introduce — a recalculated or rescaled effect size, an implied magnitude, a "this would imply that" — is itself a claim, and you must be able to derive it from the paper's own definitions and design before you rest an assessment on it. If the step only holds under an assumption the paper does not make, the error is YOURS: drop the step and assess the concern as it was actually raised.

**NOTE ON MATHEMATICAL ISSUES:** When the Blue Team classifies a math issue as Type A, check whether the Blue Team actually derived the paper's result independently or merely asserted "the paper's derivation is standard." If the Blue Team does not show its own derivation, do not accept the Type A classification.

**A badly-stated real concern is not a Red Team mistake.** You MAY recover and sharpen a genuine concern the Red Team stated poorly, and you MAY draw a conclusion the authors did not state — combining the paper's OWN premises into a new conclusion is valid synthesis and it stays. The one line you may not cross: **every premise your assessment rests on must be one the PAPER establishes, not one you supply.** You may not import a premise the paper does not make — whether that is reinterpreting what one of its quantities means, asserting that some method "is standard" or "expected", or taking an "X implies Y" step the paper does not license. If a critique only survives once you supply such a premise, the premise is yours and the critique fails: rule on the dispute as the two teams actually stated it. The test is never whether your reasoning sounds right; it is whether the paper's own content carries it. Deflecting a cheap shot is a correct deletion; it is not a verdict that the paper is sound on its actual claims.

**(2) Omissions and transparency are important.** When the Red Team says that something is missing from the text, this can be a valid concern, but you must check it yourself: an omission is only a problem if the text genuinely needs the missing element, not when its absence is an intrinsic part of the design the authors deliberately chose.

**(3) Assess Blue Team defences with the same rigour as Red Team critiques.** In particular, check whether cited textual evidence actually resolves the concern raised or merely acknowledges it. A single paragraph or sentence in the text does not necessarily constitute an adequate response to a structural concern. If the Blue Team is wrong, you can say so.

**(4) Clerical errors are minor.** Anything described as a "Type C" issue is a clerical error and must be considered minor, even if it seems substantial. Given that it is impossible for this assessment system to determine the causes or effects of "Type C" issues, they must be noted but downplayed. This applies ONLY to genuine clerical errors: if an issue is in fact structural (a "Type D" issue that threatens the logic of the text), it must NOT be downplayed as clerical — Type D structural errors are more important than Type C clerical ones.

**(5) Issues can be debatable.** For theoretical texts, in particular, there can be valid points in both the Red and Blue Teams' accounts. In this instance, you should flag them as **DEBATABLE** in your output. Explain where there are elements of truth in both the Red Team's critique and the Blue Team's defence. However, do not automatically assume that every issue is debatable.

**(6) Nuance and uncertainty is fine.** This system has limits: we cannot look at the replication files, source data, or the existing literature. There can therefore be a considerable degree of uncertainty in our assessment. **THIS IS FINE.** For example:
- When evaluating Type A (Red Team mistake) classifications (Type F has its own rule (9) below), distinguish between three outcomes: (a) the Red Team's critique is clearly wrong; (b) the text addresses the concern but the adequacy of that response is debatable; (c) the Red Team is correct. For outcome (b), classify the issue as debatable rather than accepting the Blue Team's classification outright. A single sentence or paragraph in the text acknowledging or defining something does not automatically resolve a structural concern about whether that acknowledgment or definition does sufficient analytical work. On the other hand, **do not criticize the text for any errors that are not in fact errors.**

**(7) Acknowledged issues (Type B) require careful handling.** When the Blue Team classifies an issue as Type B:

1. **Verify the acknowledgement actually exists.** Quote the paper's verbatim acknowledgement language. If the Blue Team has cited a passage that does not actually acknowledge the specific concern the Red Team raised, the Type B classification is wrong and you should reject it.

2. **Decide whether the acknowledgement is convincing.** The only question is whether the paper's acknowledgement *actually defuses the concern entirely*. A paper can explicitly acknowledge a limitation and still leave the critique with real force — when the acknowledgement is a bare admission with no resolution, when it is conceded in passing but contradicted by the headline claim, or when the critique synthesizes one or more acknowledged points into a stronger conclusion the authors did not draw. An acknowledgement is convincing only when the paper shows the limitation has been addressed in a way that leaves the critique adding no extra analytical force beyond what the authors themselves say.

3. **Recommend an action.** If the acknowledgement is convincing, recommend DELETE. If it is unconvincing, recommend KEEP — the kept paragraph must include the verbatim acknowledgement quote and make clear what the critique adds beyond what the paper says. Do not default to "keep with token acknowledgement"; equally, do not default to DELETE just because the paper used the same words.

**(8) Empirically addressed issues (Type H) demand the MOST rigorous handling on the list, because they carry the highest risk of lazy acceptance.** The existence of a robustness check, placebo, or statistical test is NOT a defence. A test defuses a concern ONLY if it neutralises the **specific causal mechanism** the concern relies on. Audit that link; do not credit the author merely for having run something. **Do NOT recommend DELETE merely because a test exists and points the author's way. Treat the claimed test as a claim to be audited, exactly as you would audit a Red Team claim.**

For every Type H issue, you MUST work through these steps in your assessment:
1. **State the mechanism.** In one sentence, name the precise causal pathway the concern relies on: the specific way the result is wrong if the concern is right. Derive this from THIS paper and THIS concern; do not import a generic template.
2. **State what the cited test actually rules out**, then compare it to that mechanism. Be exact about the difference between the threat the test closes and the threat the concern raises: a robustness check very often neutralises an *adjacent* threat while leaving the named one untouched. Decide, in terms specific to this test and this mechanism, whether the test acts on the *same* pathway you named in step 1 or merely a neighbouring one. Show your reasoning; do not assert.
3. **State the residual.** Name explicitly what the test leaves open. If anything material about the original mechanism survives, the concern is not fully defused.
4. **Recommend.** DELETE only if the test genuinely closes the named mechanism with NO material residual. DEBATABLE if it closes the mechanism only partially, mitigates it, or shifts it: give both sides and the residual. KEEP if it addresses a different threat than the one raised, or the mechanism link fails: state precisely where the test misses.

If the Blue Team's MECHANISM LINK is vague, asserted, or absent, that ALONE is grounds to reject the defence: a Type H defence with no demonstrated link to the concern's mechanism is not a defence. When in doubt, the burden is on the defence, not the critique.

**(9) Features-not-bugs (Type F) must be verified, not rubber-stamped — and a genuine one is a DELETE, not a manufactured DEBATABLE.** When the Blue Team classifies an issue as Type F — claiming the thing the Red Team attacks is an intrinsic, deliberate part of the design rather than a flaw:
1. **Verify it is genuinely intrinsic and deliberate.** Quote where the paper defines or adopts the choice. A real Type F is one where the Red Team's critique would only bite by demanding a different design, scope, sample, or target than the one the authors deliberately chose.
2. **Check the choice is defensible for the question the paper asks** — not merely that the authors made it. A deliberate choice can still be a genuine flaw if it is the wrong choice for the stated goal.
3. **Recommend.** If the choice is genuinely intrinsic AND defensible, the concern is answered — recommend DELETE; the Red Team is, in effect, asking the paper to be a different paper. If the choice is deliberate but NOT defensible for the paper's own goal, recommend KEEP and state precisely why it fails that goal. What you must NOT do is keep an intrinsic-and-defensible choice alive as DEBATABLE by supplying an argument, premise, or implication the paper and the teams did not make — that is the error the badly-stated-concern rule above forbids.
